# Part A — Human-Readable Mission Document

# 1. Mission Metadata

**Mission ID:** J2-M13
**Journey:** Make Money Grow
**Mission Title:** Assets and Liabilities
**Core Concept:** Some things can generate income or increase in value, while others mainly require ongoing spending. Their financial role may depend on how they are used.
**Target Age Group:** Kids 8–12
**Difficulty:** Beginner
**Estimated Duration:** 4 minutes
**Currency and Locale:** United Kingdom — GBP (£), en-GB
**Language:** English

**Learning Objective:**
Understand the difference between things that may generate value and things that mainly cost money.

**Primary Misconception:**
Every expensive item is automatically an asset.

**Secondary Misconceptions:**

* An item is always an asset or always a liability, regardless of how it is used.
* Anything that earns money has no costs or financial risks.

**Key Takeaway:**
The same item can play a different financial role depending on how it is used.

# 5. Main Interaction

**Interaction Type:** Sort Categories

**Interaction Title:** What Role Does It Play?

**Instruction:**
Sort each example into Can Generate Value, Mainly Costs Money, or Depends on Use. Consider income, value, purpose, and ongoing costs.

## Educational Assumptions

* These examples show simplified financial roles.
* A trusted adult manages paid work, renting, contracts, and payments.
* Earning income does not guarantee that an item will be profitable.
* Maintenance, repairs, subscriptions, and other costs must also be considered.
* An item may move to another category if its use or costs change.
* Future prices and income are not guaranteed.

## Categories

### Can Generate Value

Items that may earn income, support paid work, or provide lasting financial value under the stated conditions.

### Mainly Costs Money

Items that do not currently earn income and mainly create purchase, subscription, repair, or maintenance costs.

### Depends on Use

Items whose financial role changes depending on how they are used, what they earn, and what they cost.

## Explanation of Ambiguity

The same item can play different roles.

For example:

* A bike used only for recreation mainly creates costs.
* The same type of bike used for paid deliveries may help generate value.
* A computer used only for gaming creates costs, while one used for paid work may generate income.
* Income alone does not decide the result. Running costs, repairs, time, risks, and changing value also matter.

## Placement Rules

* Use the details provided for each example.
* Consider both money coming in and money going out.

# 6. Concept Reveal

**Reveal Title:** What You Own—and What You Owe

**Explanation:**
You sorted items by how they were used. An **asset** is something you own that has value and may generate income or rise in value. A **liability** is money you owe, such as a loan. An item that mainly creates costs is not technically always a liability, but any debt used to buy it may be. Price alone does not decide an item’s role. Income, changing value, maintenance, and purpose all matter.

**Suggested Visual:**
A balance board separates things owned, money earned, running costs, and money owed. The same bike appears in two different roles.

**Explain It Simply:**
Check what an item earns, costs, is worth, and how it is used.

**Primary Button Label:** See Another Example

# 8. Quiz

## Question 1 — Recognition

**Question:**
Which item is most likely to generate value in the situation described?

### Options

1. **A laptop used for paid design work**
2. An unused phone with a monthly subscription
3. Trainers bought only for status
4. A broken device kept in storage

**Correct Answer:**
A laptop used for paid design work

**Correct-Answer Explanation:**
The laptop supports a service that earns income, although software, repairs, and other costs must still be considered.

**Incorrect Feedback — An unused phone with a monthly subscription:**
The phone is not creating income and continues to produce a monthly cost.

**Incorrect Feedback — Trainers bought only for status:**
Their price or appearance does not automatically make them an income-producing asset.

**Incorrect Feedback — A broken device kept in storage:**
The device is not currently producing income or useful financial value.

**Misconception Being Tested:**
Anything expensive automatically creates financial value.

---

## Question 2 — Misconception

**Question:**
A watch costs £2,000. Does its high price automatically make it an asset that improves someone’s finances?

### Options

1. **No, its use, value, income, and costs still matter**
2. Yes, because every expensive item is an asset
3. Yes, because high prices always rise
4. Yes, even if it quickly loses value

**Correct Answer:**
No, its use, value, income, and costs still matter

**Correct-Answer Explanation:**
An expensive item may lose value, create costs, or produce no income. Price alone does not show whether it improves someone’s finances.

**Incorrect Feedback — Yes, because every expensive item is an asset:**
An expensive purchase can still lose value or mainly create ongoing spending.

**Incorrect Feedback — Yes, because high prices always rise:**
An item’s future price may rise, fall, or stay similar. An increase is not guaranteed.

**Incorrect Feedback — Yes, even if it quickly loses value:**
Losing value can weaken the item’s financial role even when its original price was high.

**Misconception Being Tested:**
Every expensive item is automatically an asset.

---

## Question 3 — Application

**Question:**
A family car earns £200 from paid work but costs £170 for fuel, repairs, and other work-related expenses. What should the family consider?

### Options

1. **The £30 remaining and whether other costs or risks exist**
2. Only the £200 income
3. Only the car’s purchase price
4. Assume all £200 is profit

**Correct Answer:**
The £30 remaining and whether other costs or risks exist

**Correct-Answer Explanation:**
The income must be compared with all related costs. In this simplified example, £30 remains before considering any additional expenses or risks.

**Incorrect Feedback — Only the £200 income:**
Income alone does not show the result. The £170 of costs must also be included.

**Incorrect Feedback — Only the car’s purchase price:**
The purchase price matters, but ongoing income, fuel, repairs, and other costs also affect the car’s role.

**Incorrect Feedback — Assume all £200 is profit:**
Profit is what remains after related costs. The full £200 is not profit when £170 was spent.

**Misconception Being Tested:**
Anything that earns money has no costs or financial risks.
