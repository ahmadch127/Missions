# 1. Mission Metadata

**Mission ID:** J3-M19
**Journey:** Become an Investor
**Mission Title:** Bonds
**Core Concept:** A bond is generally a loan made by an investor to a company or government. The borrower promises interest payments and repayment under agreed terms.
**Target Age Group:** Teens 13–17
**Difficulty:** Easy
**Estimated Duration:** 4 minutes
**Currency and Locale:** United Kingdom — GBP (£), en-GB
**Language:** English

**Learning Objective:**
Understand that buying a bond generally means lending money to an organisation rather than purchasing ownership in it.

**Primary Misconception:**
Bonds are the same as stocks.

**Secondary Misconceptions:**

* Buying a bond gives the investor ownership or control of the borrower.
* Bonds are completely risk-free and always repay investors on time.

**Key Takeaway:**
Buying a bond generally means lending money, not owning the borrower.

# 5. Main Interaction

**Interaction Type:** Match Pairs

**Interaction Title:** Ownership or Lending?

**Instruction:**
Match each financial term with what it represents.

## Educational Assumptions

* BrightBuild and its bond are fictional.
* The bond terms are simplified classroom examples.
* A bond is generally a lending arrangement rather than company ownership.
* Bond issuers may be companies, governments, or other organisations.
* Interest and repayment are promises under the bond terms, not guaranteed outcomes.
* A bond’s market value may rise or fall before its repayment date.
* Real bonds can have different payment schedules, risks, prices, and conditions.
* The activity does not recommend buying any real bond or stock.

## Left-Side Items

1. Stock
2. Bond
3. Dividend
4. Interest payment

## Right-Side Items

1. Ownership in a company
2. Money lent to a company or government
3. A possible distribution of company profits to shareholders
4. An amount paid to a lender for borrowing money

## Correct Pairs

### Stock → Ownership in a Company

**Explanation:**
Buying stock generally makes the investor a partial owner. The stock’s value may rise or fall, and profit is not guaranteed.

### Bond → Money Lent to a Company or Government

**Explanation:**
Buying a bond generally means lending money to the issuer under agreed repayment and interest terms.

### Dividend → A Possible Distribution of Company Profits to Shareholders

**Explanation:**
Some companies may distribute part of their profits to shareholders. Dividends are not guaranteed.

### Interest Payment → An Amount Paid to a Lender for Borrowing Money

**Explanation:**
Interest is the amount the borrower agrees to pay for using the lender’s money.

## Feedback for Incorrect Matching

### Stock Matched With Money Lent

A stock usually represents ownership. Lending money to an organisation is more closely connected with a bond.

### Stock Matched With Dividend

A dividend may be paid to a shareholder, but the stock itself represents ownership.

### Stock Matched With Interest Payment

Interest is generally paid to a lender. A shareholder may instead receive a dividend, although dividends are not guaranteed.

### Bond Matched With Ownership

A bond generally makes the investor a lender, not a company owner.

### Bond Matched With Dividend

Dividends are connected with shares and company ownership. Bonds may make interest payments under their terms.

### Bond Matched With Interest Payment

Interest is a payment connected with a bond, but the bond itself represents the money lent.

### Dividend Matched With Money Lent

A dividend is a possible payment to shareholders rather than the original money lent.

### Dividend Matched With Interest Payment

Both may provide income, but dividends come from company decisions while bond interest follows the bond’s agreed terms.

### Interest Payment Matched With Ownership

Receiving interest does not normally create company ownership.

### Interest Payment Matched With Dividend

Interest is paid for borrowing money. A dividend is a possible distribution to shareholders.

## Classroom Bond Example

BrightBuild issues a fictional bond with these terms:

* **Amount lent by investor:** £100
* **Interest payment:** £4 each year
* **Length:** Three years
* **Promised repayment at the end:** £100

These figures explain the bond structure only. They are not a real offer or guaranteed result.

## Follow-Up Question 1

What does the investor own after buying this bond?

### Options

1. **A lending claim under the bond agreement**
2. Part of BrightBuild
3. BrightBuild’s equipment
4. Control of the company

**Correct Answer:**
A lending claim under the bond agreement

**Explanation:**
The investor has lent £100 under agreed terms. The bond does not normally provide company ownership.

## Follow-Up Question 2

If BrightBuild makes every promised payment, how much would the investor receive over three years?

### Options

1. £100
2. **£112**
3. £104
4. £300

**Correct Answer:**
£112

**Explanation:**
The investor receives three £4 interest payments, totalling £12, plus the £100 repayment.

## Follow-Up Question 3

Is receiving the full £112 guaranteed?

### Options

1. **No, the borrower may struggle to make payments**
2. Yes, every bond is risk-free
3. Yes, because interest is promised
4. No, because bonds never repay investors

**Correct Answer:**
No, the borrower may struggle to make payments

**Explanation:**
The issuer promises to pay, but financial problems could cause delayed, reduced, or missed payments.

## Bond Risk Reminder

A bond may involve several risks:

* **Repayment risk:** The issuer may struggle to make interest payments or repay the borrowed amount.
* **Price risk:** The bond’s market value may rise or fall if it is sold before repayment.
* **Interest-rate risk:** Changes in wider interest rates may affect the bond’s market value.
* **Inflation risk:** Future payments may buy less if prices rise.

These risks vary between bonds.

# 6. Concept Reveal

**Reveal Title:** Lending Instead of Owning

**Explanation:**
You matched a bond with money lent to a company or government. Buying a **bond** generally makes you a lender, not an owner. The issuer promises interest payments and repayment under agreed terms. A stock represents ownership and may provide dividends, while a bond creates a repayment claim. Bonds may sometimes move less sharply than some stocks, but they are not risk-free. Payments can be missed, and the bond’s market value can change.

**Suggested Visual:**
A stock certificate connects to an ownership badge, while a bond connects to a lending agreement and payment calendar.

**Explain It Simply:**
Stocks create ownership; bonds generally create a lending relationship.
